# THE EXPAT NETWORK — Social Content Source Stack

**Status:** Canonical working source policy for social-content research
**Primary use:** Founder LinkedIn and TEN LinkedIn Page
**Geographic positioning:** Europe-first

---

## 1. Purpose

This file defines how TEN researches, verifies and classifies evidence for social content.

The objective is not to collect the largest possible source list. The objective is to identify the strongest applicable evidence for a specific editorial question while protecting TEN from:

- outdated or inaccurate claims;
- Germany-only drift;
- unsupported labour-shortage conclusions;
- overclaiming employer openness;
- accidental legal or regulated advice;
- weak secondary sourcing;
- AI-generated factual errors;
- platform-algorithm folklore presented as fact.

---

## 2. Europe-First Source Principle

Every content idea must be classified before sourcing begins as one of:

1. **EU-COMPARATIVE** — a Europe-level or cross-border question;
2. **CORRIDOR** — a defined source-country → destination-country/region question;
3. **COUNTRY DEEP DIVE** — a question about one national labour market.

The geographic scope is decided **before** sources are selected.

For Europe-level questions, start with European comparative and cross-border evidence.

For country-level questions, use the relevant national official-source stack.

Germany is a priority and deep-evidence market, but it is **not the default editorial frame**.

A source can be factually strong and still create strategic drift if its repeated use causes TEN to present itself as a Germany-only labour-market account.

During the initial LinkedIn test period, no more than two of any five consecutive Founder posts should be Germany-primary unless a material current development justifies a documented exception.

---

## 3. Layer A — European Comparative Primary Sources

Use these first for EU-COMPARATIVE questions.

Core source classes include:

- European Labour Authority (ELA);
- EURES;
- Eurostat;
- European Commission;
- CEDEFOP;
- European Migration Network (EMN);
- OECD where directly relevant;
- EU Talent Pool for policy and structural-development context.

### EU Talent Pool scope

Until operational matching and outcome data are available, treat the EU Talent Pool as a **policy / structural-development source**, not as evidence of current placement volumes, matching success or corridor outcomes.

### Typical questions Layer A can answer

- Where do shortage and surplus classifications differ across Europe?
- Which skills or occupations show cross-country demand signals?
- What mobility barriers are identified across Member States?
- What European labour-migration policy changes are material?
- What long-term skills forecasts are emerging?

---

## 4. Layer B — National Primary Sources

Use national sources when the question is country-specific or when an EU-level finding needs national validation.

The relevant national stack must be selected dynamically by country.

### Germany example

- Bundesagentur für Arbeit (BA);
- IAB;
- Destatis;
- Zentrale Auslands- und Fachvermittlung (ZAV);
- Make it in Germany;
- Anerkennung in Deutschland;
- BIBB recognition monitoring;
- competent recognition bodies;
- federal ministries, BAMF and German diplomatic missions where applicable.

### Important source boundaries

**BA-X** is a labour-demand / vacancy-pressure indicator only. It is not evidence of visa sponsorship, non-EU eligibility or employer willingness to hire internationally.

**Make it in Germany** can be a useful indicator of international recruitment intent when employers use official channels designed to reach professionals abroad, but this does not prove willingness to hire every non-EU candidate or guarantee work-permit support.

**ZAV** is relevant for international placement, recruitment programmes and cross-border labour-market activity, but individual corridor conclusions still require evidence.

### Future national stacks

When TEN researches France, the Netherlands, Ireland, Austria or other European markets, identify and use the equivalent national official authorities rather than defaulting to German sources.

---

## 5. Layer C — Original Research

Use original research to answer questions official sources do not measure well.

Eligible source classes include:

- peer-reviewed or academic research;
- reputable research institutes;
- original employer surveys;
- sector studies;
- university labour-market research;
- employer studies with transparent methodology;
- original datasets with disclosed sampling and limitations.

Prefer the original study over a press article summarising the study.

Do not use an unsupported percentage from a consultancy or marketing article when the underlying methodology cannot be inspected.

---

## 6. Layer D — Reality / Journalism Radar

Use reputable journalism for discovery, context and emerging user pain points.

Examples include:

- The Local;
- Reuters;
- reputable national press;
- specialist employment, migration or mobility publications.

### The Local — Expat Reality Radar

TEN may use The Local Germany as an **Expat Reality Radar** because the founder has paid access.

Primary uses:

- discover recurring concerns among English-speaking internationals;
- identify bureaucracy and workplace friction;
- identify salary and language questions;
- detect recognition and residence pain points;
- identify emerging policy stories;
- discover questions worth verifying through primary sources.

Preferred workflow:

**The Local article → pain point / claim → original or official source → verification → original TEN interpretation**

The Local is not a substitute for official immigration, recognition, labour-law or labour-market sources.

### Audience-bias caveat

The Local readership should not be treated as statistically representative of prospective non-EU candidates, Indian professionals, employers or the wider European labour market.

Its value is directional and narrative, not population-level inference.

### Comment-thread firewall

Comments below journalism articles are Layer E anecdotal signals, not Layer D journalism.

Never cite a reader comment as if it were part of the reported article.

---

## 7. Layer E — Market Signals

Market signals include:

- employer comments;
- recruiter conversations;
- candidate experiences;
- LinkedIn posts and comments;
- institutional conversations;
- specialist practitioner observations.

Treat them as one of:

- Anecdote;
- Directional Signal;
- Repeated Signal;
- Validated Pattern.

One comment or DM is never market validation.

Signals become stronger when they repeat independently across employers, recruiters, candidates, institutions or additional evidence sources.

Repeated signals should generate new research questions for the next Weekly Intelligence Pack.

---

## 8. Layer F — AI

AI systems may be used for:

- research discovery;
- query generation;
- source triage;
- synthesis;
- drafting;
- counterarguments;
- QA;
- structure and editing.

AI output is never itself a factual source.

Any AI-generated claim must be traceable to an applicable source before publication.

---

## 9. Employer-Openness Evidence Model

There is no single authoritative index measuring employer willingness to recruit non-EU professionals.

TEN therefore treats employer openness as a **composite signal**.

Potential evidence includes:

1. explicit international-recruitment statements;
2. vacancies intentionally promoted through international recruitment channels;
3. participation in ZAV or other verified international recruitment programmes;
4. explicit language flexibility;
5. explicit relocation or work-permit support;
6. repeated direct employer or recruiter conversations;
7. visible international-hiring history;
8. workforce evidence showing relevant international recruitment;
9. actual documented hiring outcomes.

A corridor must not be called actionable merely because a shortage or vacancy exists.

Employer openness must be described according to the strength of the available evidence.

---

## 10. Recognition Evidence Stack

Recognition questions should use more than the general recognition portal where possible.

Relevant sources include:

- Anerkennung in Deutschland;
- Destatis statistics on foreign professional-qualification recognition;
- BIBB recognition monitoring and analysis;
- competent profession-specific recognition bodies;
- applicable national authorities in non-German destination markets.

Do not infer individual recognition outcomes from aggregate statistics.

---

## 11. Search-Demand Discovery

Google Trends may be used to identify relative search-interest patterns, emerging queries and geographical interest.

Google Trends is **not** labour-demand evidence and is **not** labour-supply evidence.

Search interest from candidates does not prove employer demand.

Search interest from a country does not prove migration intention, eligibility or hiring feasibility.

---

## 12. Platform-Strategy Evidence Taxonomy

The same evidence discipline applies to claims about LinkedIn itself.

### Platform-Primary Verified

LinkedIn explicitly documents the feature, metric or behaviour.

### Observational Benchmark

A credible independent dataset reports a pattern.

### Practitioner Hypothesis

A creator, vendor or practitioner reports directional behaviour that is not independently verified by LinkedIn.

### Unsupported Folklore

Claims such as universal golden hours, guaranteed first-hour boosts, link penalties or fixed carousel-slide rules without adequate evidence.

TEN may test observational or practitioner hypotheses internally.

Do not present them publicly as settled facts about LinkedIn's algorithm.

---

## 13. Internal Claim Status

Every substantive factual claim should receive one internal status:

### PRIMARY-VERIFIED

Supported by the strongest applicable current official or primary source.

### SECONDARY-SUPPORTED

Supported by credible secondary or original research but not yet confirmed through the strongest applicable primary source.

### NOT-YET-PRIMARY-VERIFIED

Potentially useful but requires stronger verification before publication as fact.

### UNSUPPORTED

Not suitable for publication as a factual claim.

High-volatility or regulated claims must be re-verified immediately before publication where material.

---

## 14. Attribution and Trust Rules

TEN must:

- distinguish interpretation from official determination;
- attribute material data and research accurately;
- use exact dates where time sensitivity matters;
- avoid implying endorsement, partnership or cooperation;
- avoid copying or closely paraphrasing journalism;
- avoid converting anecdotes into broad market claims;
- record verification dates for high-volatility claims;
- route users to official or qualified sources where required.

---

## 15. Source-Selection Test

Before using a source, ask:

1. What exact question are we answering?
2. Is it Europe-level, corridor-level or country-level?
3. Is this the strongest applicable source?
4. Is the evidence current enough?
5. Does the source actually support the claim being made?
6. What does the source not prove?
7. Could repeated use of this source distort TEN's Europe-first positioning?
8. Does a current official or original source need to be checked before publication?

If the answer is unclear, do not publish the claim as fact.
